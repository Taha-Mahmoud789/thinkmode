In the early 2000s, Wi-Fi meant 802.11b — 11 Mbps if you were lucky, less if a microwave or a wall got involved. The race to the next tier was on, and most of the industry lined up behind the IEEE's orderly progression: 802.11a, then g, then n. Context for this period is well-documented externally — e.g., [IEEE Computer Society](https://www.computer.org/) and [ACM Digital Library](https://dl.acm.org/) provide the baseline against which the computing claim should be judged. Cross-check with a third source before operationalizing.

Airgo walked in with a different idea. Its chipset claimed "true" next-generation wireless LAN performance using **MIMO** — multiple antennas transmitting multiple streams at once. The promise: throughput that didn't collapse the moment you left the same room as the router.

## Why MIMO was a bet, not a sure thing

MIMO (multiple-input, multiple-output) sounded like magic and behaved like math. Instead of fighting multipath interference — signals bouncing off walls and arriving late — MIMO *used* it, turning reflected signals into extra data lanes.

The catch was timing:

- The standard (802.11n) wasn't finalized. Shipping MIMO early meant betting on a future you didn't control.
- Channel modeling in real homes is messy. Labs love MIMO; stairwells and concrete don't.
- Silicon maturity mattered. A great algorithm in a hot, expensive chip isn't a product.

Airgo was essentially saying: *the standard is too slow, trust the physics.*

## The lesson that survived

MIMO won. It's in every modern router, phone, and laptop. But Airgo as a company? Absorbed. The technology was right; the route to market — ahead of the standard, alone — was the hard part.

> Standards bodies move at the speed of consensus. Breakthroughs move at the speed of physics. The gap between them is where startups die.

## The 2026 echo

Every few years a wireless startup claims the next leap — Wi-Fi 7, 6G, terahertz. The pattern repeats: the physics is often sound, the standards lag, and the survivors are the ones who either shaped the standard or had the balance sheet to wait it out.

Airgo's chipset was a preview of a truth the industry keeps relearning: **being right about the technology is necessary. Being right about the timeline is what keeps the lights on.**
