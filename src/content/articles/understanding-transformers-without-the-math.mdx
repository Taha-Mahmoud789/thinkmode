You do not need calculus to understand what a transformer does. You need a good map. This is that map — every core idea, translated into plain language, with none of the usual hand-waving.

## Tokens: the atoms of text

Models cannot read letters or words. Text is chopped into **tokens** — frequent chunks of characters — and each token is looked up in a giant table that returns its **embedding**: a list of numbers encoding meaning. "Bank" near "river" lives somewhere different than "bank" near "loan." Meaning becomes geometry.

## Attention: highlighting, mechanically

The transformer's core trick is letting every token look at every other token and decide what matters. For each token, the model asks three questions built from its embedding:

- **Query:** what am I looking for?
- **Key:** what do I offer?
- **Value:** what do I actually pass along?

Imagine reading *"The trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because it was too big."* To resolve "it," the token runs its query against every key. "Trophy" scores highest — so the model blends in trophy's value, and "it" effectively means trophy downstream. Do this across dozens of parallel **attention heads**, each free to track different relationships — grammar, references, topic — and you have multi-head attention.

> Attention is a learned highlighter. It never copies words; it mixes meanings, weighted by relevance, hundreds of layers deep.

## Stacking: depth creates abstraction

One attention layer notices surface patterns. Stack dozens, and earlier layers feed later ones: low-level syntax at the bottom, discourse and intent near the top. Each layer also passes its result through a small per-token neural network — time to digest before the next round of looking-around.

## Why bigger context windows changed everything

Early models saw a paragraph. Modern ones see entire books, which turns attention into something like long-term working memory: cite page 400 while answering about page 3. But attention cost grows quadratically — double the tokens, four times the work — which is why long-context was an engineering race and why techniques like sliding windows and sparse attention exist at all.

## Generation: one token at a time

Everything above describes *understanding*. Output is simpler and stranger: the model predicts the next token from everything so far, appends it, and repeats. All the reasoning you observe emerges from next-token prediction executed extremely well, with two control knobs:

- **Temperature** — randomness. Low: deterministic, repetitive. High: creative, chaotic.
- **Top-p** — the candidate pool size. Together they trade surprise against coherence.

<Callout variant="info" title="Why hallucinations happen">
Nothing in this loop distinguishes true from fluent. The model continues plausible text. When your documents contradict its priors, plausibility can win — which is exactly why retrieval grounding and citations matter in real systems.
</Callout>

## What the math adds (when you want it)

When you are ready, the formal versions are refinements, not revelations: queries/keys/values become learned matrices; "blending" becomes softmax-weighted sums; training becomes gradient descent on billions of predictions. The intuition transfers intact.

Until then, hold onto the map: **embed meaning into geometry, let tokens attend to what matters, stack depth until abstractions emerge, generate one confident guess at a time.** Every modern AI system you use — chatbots, copilots, image generators' text halves — is some variation of that sentence scaled up until it started working.
