The early-2000s vision of the Semantic Web was deceptively simple: don't just publish documents; publish *meaning*. Tag data with structured relationships — this is a person, that is a price, this event happens on a date — so software could connect, query, and reason across sites without a human in the loop. Context for this period is well-documented externally — e.g., [MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/) and [NIST Technology](https://www.nist.gov/topics/technology) provide the baseline against which the technology claim should be judged. Cross-check with a third source before operationalizing.

It was, in hindsight, a bet on **machine-readable knowledge** years before that phrase became fashionable.

## Why it stalled (and why that's okay)

The Semantic Web didn't fail so much as it fragmented:

- **Standards friction.** RDF, OWL, and SPARQL were powerful and heavy — more than most publishers wanted to learn.
- **Incentive gap.** Marking up meaning is work; the payoff was diffuse and collective.
- **The human web won.** Plain HTML plus search engines solved "find stuff" well enough that few felt the pinch.

So the grand unified knowledge graph didn't arrive on schedule. But pieces of it did.

- **Schema.org** tags that tell Google what your page *is*.
- **Knowledge panels** built from structured data, not just crawled text.
- **APIs and JSON** — the de facto semantic layer for apps talking to apps.
- **Knowledge graphs** inside every major platform.

> The Semantic Web didn't die. It got absorbed into the plumbing.

## The 2026 echo

Today's AI models do a crude version of what the Semantic Web wanted: extract meaning from messy text. The irony is that structured data would make them vastly more reliable — and we're slowly rediscovering the value of *labeling* our information instead of hoping models infer it.

The future of human knowledge was never going to be a single global ontology. It's the quieter, messier layering of meaning we keep rebuilding — one schema at a time.
